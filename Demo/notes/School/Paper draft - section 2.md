## 2.1 The cold start on a campus

Every two-sided marketplace starts with the same chicken-or-egg problem. Sellers won't list until there are buyers, and buyers won't open the app until there's something to buy. The usual story is that a network has to reach critical mass before network effects do any work for you.

A campus should be the easiest place to solve this. The network is tiny and dense. Everyone shares an email domain, a dining hall and the same two weeks a semester when textbooks change hands. Facebook used exactly this. It opened at Harvard in February 2004 and reached Columbia, Stanford and Yale within about a month, one college at a time, and each campus was its own small network before the next one opened.

So why do campus marketplaces stall at zero? My argument is that the atomic network on a campus isn't the campus. It's smaller than that. It's a residential college, or two adjacent ones, where a handoff is a two-minute walk. Launching to all of Yale at once spreads the first fifty listings across fourteen colleges and nobody finds anything near them.

The same pattern shows up in city marketplaces. You launch one dense neighbourhood, you guarantee the supply side's earnings until demand catches up, and you measure fill rate instead of signups. The campus version is to seed listings by hand in one college and measure how many get a swap request within 72 hours.

## 2.2 Why it never tips: multi-homing on free channels

Even a liquid college has a leak. Students already post in GroupMe chats and the class Facebook group, and both cost nothing. A seller who lists in three places takes the first answer from anywhere, so the new marketplace never gets credit for the match. When both sides multi-home, the cheapest channel wins every tie.

Evidence so far is thin. Chen, Chevalier, Rossi and Oehlsen on Uber drivers is about the value of flexible hours, which is next door to multi-homing but not the same thing. Need something cleaner.
