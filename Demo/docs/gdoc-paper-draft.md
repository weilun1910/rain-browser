# Stuck at zero: why campus marketplaces stall

Theo Park. Platforms and Markets, Prof. Hartley. Draft, not for circulation.

## 1. Introduction

Every college has a graveyard of student-built marketplaces. Textbook exchanges, sublet boards, furniture swaps at move-out. Most launch with a burst of enthusiasm in September and are quiet by November. Meanwhile the same trades keep happening anyway, in GroupMe chats and free-and-for-sale Facebook groups that nobody designed and nobody runs.

This is strange if you take the theory of two-sided markets at face value. A campus is close to an ideal setting for a marketplace. Buyers and sellers live within a few minutes of each other. They share a verified email domain. Demand is predictable, because every student needs books in the same two weeks of every term. Facebook famously used campuses as its launch pads in 2004 for exactly these reasons.

This paper argues that campus marketplaces fail at the very beginning, not later on. They never become liquid, because they launch to the whole campus when the network that actually matters is far smaller. And they never tip, because both sides keep using free channels at the same time, and when everyone multi-homes the free channel wins every tie.

Section 2 develops this argument. Section 3 looks at what is and isn't different about a campus. Section 4 uses a pilot of Swapshelf, a textbook and dorm gear swap app, in two Yale residential colleges as a case. Section 5 checks the argument against Rochet and Tirole's account of price structure in two-sided markets.
